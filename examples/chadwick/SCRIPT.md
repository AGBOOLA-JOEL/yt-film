## P01 · The note
`[SHOTS: S01a_fifth_ave_lines → S01a_fifth_ave (draws itself, then the street comes alive), S01b_dillon_waits]` `[LABEL: "New York · around 1902"]` `[FX: chimney smoke, horse breath, gas flames, snow; the carriage lamp]`
New York, around nineteen-oh-two. A Cleveland lawyer named Dillon is waiting in a carriage on Fifth Avenue, checking his watch. The woman he drove here has been inside Andrew Carnegie's mansion for nearly half an hour.

## P02 · The paper
`[COMIC: S01c_doorway, S01d_trip, S01e_paper_falls, S01f_reads]` `[FX: the note slips from the muff and lands (Lift)]`
When she finally comes out, she turns at the door to wave goodbye to someone inside. On the carriage step she stumbles, and a folded paper slips from her muff onto the pavement. Dillon picks it up.

## P03 · Two million dollars
`[SHOTS: S01g_dillon_face, S01f_reads (the words write themselves), S01h_cassie_face]` `[NOTE 1: the carriage story is Dillon's own account]`
It is a promissory note. I promise to pay, to the order of Cassie L. Chadwick, two million dollars. And at the bottom, a signature. Andrew Carnegie. She takes it back from him, and lowers her eyes.

## P04 · The promise
`[SHOTS: S01i_drive_off, S12i_pavement → PAGE: B01_note (the note, written; FORGED stamps on it)]`
It was a forgery. The woman who dropped it, on purpose, had been forging things since she was thirteen. For the next two years, bankers across Ohio would lend her millions on the strength of that one name, without ever looking inside the envelope that was supposed to prove it. This is how she did it.

## P05 · Betsy
`[SHOTS: S02a_town, S02b_father]` `[DRAW: X01.1 the railway handcar → on S02b's sky, card wash]` `[LABEL: "Ontario · 1857"]` `[PAGE: B03_map — the Great Lakes draw in; a dot in Ontario]` `[FX: chimney smoke, snow]`
She was born Elizabeth Bigley, in eighteen fifty-seven, in a small town in Ontario, Canada. Her father, Daniel, worked on the railway, keeping the track in repair. The family called her Betsy.

## P06 · The quiet one
`[SHOTS: S02c_kitchen, S02d_betsy_face]` `[DRAW: X01.0 farmhouse, X01.3 the girl → on S02c's wall, card wash]` `[FX: the oil lamp]`
As a girl she lost the hearing in one ear, and she spoke with an impediment, so she said very little. People who knew her later remembered a child who watched, and listened, and kept her words to herself.

## P07 · The first letter
`[SHOTS: S02e_letter, S02f_letter_d]` `[DRAW: X01.2 the oil lamp and pen → beside S02f; X02.0 ink and pen, X02.1 the two letters]` `[FX: lamp flame, pen sound]`
At thirteen, the story goes, she wrote a letter saying that an uncle had died and left her a little money. It wasn't true. But it was the first time she learned that paper, with the right words on it, could make people believe almost anything.

## P08 · The heiress
`[SHOTS: S02g_woodstock, S02h_card]` `[LABEL: "Woodstock, Ontario · 1879"]` `[PAGE: B02_card — "Miss Bigley, Heiress to $15,000" types itself]` `[DRAW: X02.2 the calling card]` `[NOTE 2: the calling card]`
In eighteen seventy-nine, in the town of Woodstock, Ontario, she was arrested for passing forged notes. In her purse the police found her calling card. It read: Miss Bigley. Heiress to fifteen thousand dollars.

## P09 · The faces
`[SHOTS: S02i_courtroom, S02j_face_pull]` `[FX: the jurors lean back; a stamp NOT GUILTY]`
At her trial she pleaded insanity, and reportedly pulled strange faces at the jury. It worked. The jury found her not guilty. She was twenty-one.

## P10 · Cleveland
`[SHOTS: S02k_train, S03a_cleveland]` `[PAGE: B03_map — the route inks from Woodstock to Cleveland]` `[FX: train steam, mill smoke, snow]`
Her family sent her away, to live with her married sister, Alice, in Cleveland, Ohio. Cleveland was a city of iron and oil and new money. It suited her.

## P11 · Alice's furniture
`[SHOTS: S03b_furniture, S03c_alice_face]`
She borrowed money against her sister's furniture, without asking. When the family found out, she had to leave.

## P12 · Madame DeVere
`[SHOTS: S03d_garden_st, S03e_seance, S03f_crystal]` `[DRAW: X03.0 the crystal ball]` `[PAGE: B02_card — "Madame Lydia DeVere, clairvoyant"]` `[FX: candle flames and their light; incense smoke]`
So she became somebody else. In a rented room on Garden Street, under the name Madame Lydia DeVere, she told fortunes. The widows of Cleveland came to hear their futures, and paid in silver.

## P13 · Twelve days
`[SHOTS: S03g_wedding, S03h_paper, S03i_thrown_out, S03j_lydia_face]` `[DRAW: X03.2 the wedding ring]` `[LABEL: "21 November 1882"]`
In eighteen eighty-two, a doctor named Wallace Springsteen married her. Then the Cleveland newspaper printed who she really was, and her creditors arrived at his door. The marriage lasted less than two weeks.

## P14 · The names
`[SHOTS: S03k_hats, S04a_farm, S04b_hoover]` `[DRAW: X03.1 the veiled hat, X03.3 the trunk of hats]` `[PAGE: B02_card — each name crossed out and the next typed: Marie LaRose · Mrs. Hoover]`
She simply put on another name. According to one account, she was Marie LaRose for a while, and married a farmer. Then she was Mrs. Hoover, a widow in black, with a baby son named Emil.

## P15 · Toledo
`[SHOTS: S04c_toledo_shop, S04d_toledo_court]` `[LABEL: "Toledo · 1889"]` `[FX: stamp 9½ YEARS]`
In eighteen eighty-nine, in Toledo, she paid for her shopping with notes signed in the name of a respected Youngstown man. He had never seen them. This time, the court did not believe she was mad. Nine and a half years, for forgery.

## P16 · The penitentiary
`[SHOTS: S04e_pen, S04f_sewing, S04g_sew_face]` `[DRAW: X04.0 the wall, X04.1 the sewing machine, X04.2 the keys]` `[PHOTO: ohio_pen.jpg]` `[FX: chimney smoke over the walls, the gate's bolt]`
She was taken to the Ohio Penitentiary in Columbus, a grey stone fortress. She worked, and she waited, and she behaved.

## P17 · Parole
`[SHOTS: S04h_parole, S04i_gate]` `[DRAW: X04.3 the carpetbag]` `[NOTE 3: Governor McKinley]` `[FX: leaves blowing]`
After about four years, she was paroled, by the governor of Ohio. She walked out of the gate with a carpetbag, and went straight back to Cleveland.

## P18 · The house on the west side
`[SHOTS: S05a_westside, S05b_parlour, S05c_leroy_face]` `[FX: lamplit windows, rain on the street]`
There, as Mrs. Hoover, she ran a house on the city's west side, a house where young women entertained gentlemen. One evening a widowed doctor called there. His name was Leroy Chadwick. He was kind, well off, and a little lonely, and he slowly realised what kind of house he was in.

## P19 · The faint
`[SHOTS: S05d_faint, S05e_explains]`
She fainted, beautifully. When she came round, she reportedly told him she had no idea what went on there. She was only teaching the girls their manners. He believed her.

## P20 · Mrs. Chadwick
`[SHOTS: S05f_wedding, S05g_euclid_lines → S05g_euclid (draws itself), S05h_arrives]` `[LABEL: "Euclid Avenue · 1897"]` `[PHOTO: chadwick_house.jpg]` `[PAGE: B02_card — "Mrs. Cassie L. Chadwick"]`
In eighteen ninety-seven they married, and she moved into his house on Euclid Avenue, the street Cleveland called Millionaires' Row. Rockefeller had lived on it. Betsy Bigley, the railwayman's daughter, was now Mrs. Cassie Chadwick.

## P21 · Nobody came
`[SHOTS: S05i_neighbours, S06a_tea, S06b_hurt_face, S06c_cards, S06d_window, S06e_dusk]` `[FX: the tall clock ticking; snow at dusk]`
But Euclid Avenue knew a stranger when it saw one. The neighbours turned their backs. When she invited them, they didn't come. Behind their curtains, the ladies of the Row watched her house, and waited for her to give up. She didn't. She was planning something much bigger.

## P22 · The chance meeting
`[COMIC: S07a_holland, S07b_lobby, S07c_favour, S07d_ride, S07e_mansion, S07f_hall]` `[LABEL: "New York · around 1902"]`
In New York, in the lobby of a grand hotel, she bumped into a man she knew from Cleveland. It looked like chance. It wasn't. It was Dillon, the lawyer. She asked him a small favour: would he ride with her up Fifth Avenue? She had a call to make.

## P23 · Inside the house
`[SHOTS: S07g_housekeeper_face, S07h_muff, S07i_wave]` `[DRAW: X05.3 the mansion front → on S07e]`
Inside Carnegie's house, she never met Carnegie. She asked the housekeeper about a maid who had once worked there, and whether her references were any good. For nearly half an hour, she talked about nothing. Then she slipped a folded paper out of her muff, walked out, and turned at the door to wave.

## P24 · The secret
`[SHOTS: S07j_secret, S07k_cassie_tears, S07l_dillon_oath]` `[DRAW: X05.0 Carnegie → beside S07j, card wash; X02.3 the ribboned bundle of notes, "$7,000,000"]` `[PHOTO: carnegie_1905.jpg]`
In the carriage, with tears in her eyes, she told Dillon the secret he must never repeat. She was Andrew Carnegie's daughter, born out of wedlock. Out of guilt, he had given her notes like this one: seven million dollars more of them, at home, and when he died, she would inherit four hundred million. Dillon swore he would tell no one.

## P25 · The richest man in America
`[DRAW: X05.1 the steel converter, X05.2 a Carnegie library → on B03_map beside Pittsburgh]` `[PHOTO: carnegie_mansion_1902.jpg]` `[FX: Bessemer sparks]`
It was a perfect lie, because nobody could check it. Carnegie had made his fortune in steel, and was giving it away in libraries all over the world. He hadn't married until he was fifty-one. And no banker in Ohio was ever going to knock on his door and ask him whether he had a secret daughter.

## P26 · The envelope
`[SHOTS: S08a_bank, S08b_office, S08c_seal]` `[LABEL: "Wade Park Bank · Cleveland"]` `[FX: streetcar, snow; the wax seal with a little heat shimmer]`
Back in Cleveland, Dillon did exactly what she hoped. He took her to the Wade Park Bank, and to its secretary and treasurer, a careful banker named Iri Reynolds. She handed over a large brown envelope of what she said were Carnegie securities. Reynolds sealed it with wax.

## P27 · The receipt
`[SHOTS: S08d_receipt, S08e_vault, S08f_reynolds_face]` `[PAGE: B05_envelope — "NOT TO BE OPENED" types itself; a tally of months begins]` `[NOTE 4: "Iri", not "Ira"]` `[FX: the vault door's bolt]`
He wrote her a receipt, and put the envelope in the vault. Nobody opened it. Why would they? Iri Reynolds had a bank's receipt for millions of dollars of Andrew Carnegie's paper, and he was proud to guard it.

## P28 · The whisper
`[SHOTS: S08g_club, S08h_boardroom]` `[FX: cigar smoke curling (Smoke)]`
And Dillon, who had sworn to tell no one, told one banker, in confidence. Who told another. A secret like that travels fast among men who lend money.

## P29 · How it worked
`[SHOTS: S08i_desk, S08j_banker_face]` `[PAGE: B05_envelope — the chain drawn on its front: DRAW X06.0 envelope → X06.1 vault → X06.2 deposit box → X06.3 banknotes, arrows, labels; a loop arrow: "again"]`
This is how it worked. She never showed anyone the notes. She showed them the receipt. A receipt from a respectable bank, for papers signed by the richest man in America. The bankers lent her money against it, at high interest, sometimes with a little bonus for themselves. If she was Carnegie's heir, they would be paid back many times over. And then she took the same receipt to the next banker.

## P30 · Oberlin
`[SHOTS: S09a_oberlin, S09b_beckwith_office, S09c_beckwith_face, S09d_counting]` `[LABEL: "Oberlin, Ohio"]` `[FX: snow, students' breath]`
One of her best customers was a small bank in the college town of Oberlin, the Citizens National Bank. Its president, Charles Beckwith, was dazzled. His cashier, a man named Spear, counted out the money.

## P31 · His own money
`[SHOTS: S09e_own_money]` `[FX: the desk lamp, the pen]`
Beckwith lent her the bank's money. Then, alone at his desk, he lent her his own: more than a hundred thousand dollars of his savings.

## P32 · The ledger
`[PAGE: B04_ledger — the loans written in bank by bank; a running total climbs (Counter) to "about $2,000,000"]` `[DRAW: X07.0 the necklace, X07.1 the organ, X07.2 the gown → on the ledger's left page]`
Bank after bank, the numbers grew. By the end she had borrowed about two million dollars. In today's money, something like seventy million.

## P33 · Queen of Ohio
`[SHOTS: S09f_jeweller, S09g_dressmaker, S09h_closets, S09i_organ]` `[FX: the jeweller's lamp glinting on the diamonds (sparkle), the organ's sound]`
And she spent it. Diamond necklaces. Enough clothes to fill thirty closets. A pipe organ, covered in gold, in her parlour.

## P34 · The parties
`[SHOTS: S09j_dinner_lines → S09j_dinner (draws itself), S09k_gift, S09l_christmas, S09m_queen_face, S09n_night]` `[DRAW: X07.3 the carriage]` `[FX: chandelier, candles, the fireplace; walla at dinner]`
She gave lavish dinners, and, reportedly, lavish presents. The papers called her the Queen of Ohio. And now, people came. For two years, every window on Euclid Avenue's strangest house was lit.

## P35 · Newton
`[SHOTS: S10a_brookline, S10b_newton, S10c_court_clerk]` `[LABEL: "Brookline, Massachusetts · November 1904"]` `[FX: shatter from S09n into S10a; falling leaves]`
It ended with one banker who wasn't from Ohio. Herbert Newton, of Brookline, Massachusetts, had lent her a hundred and ninety thousand dollars. When he heard how much she owed to everybody else, he asked for it back. She couldn't pay. In November nineteen-oh-four, he sued.

## P36 · Carnegie
`[SHOTS: S10d_telegram, S10e_reporters, S10f_carnegie_office, S10g_carnegie_face]` `[PAGE: B06_telegram — the statement types itself]` `[DRAW: X08.0 the telegram envelope, X08.3 the press camera → on S10e]` `[FX: press camera flashes]`
Reporters went to the one man who could settle it. Carnegie's answer was short. He did not know Mrs. Chadwick of Cleveland. And he had not signed a note in more than thirty years.

## P37 · The run
`[SHOTS: S10h_run, S10i_empty, S10j_beckwith_face]` `[DRAW: X08.1 the bank with its crowd → on B03_map at Oberlin]` `[FX: snow, chimney smoke, crowd walla; the bank doors rattling]`
In Oberlin, the town ran to its bank. Farmers, shopkeepers and students crowded the doors with their bank books. But the money was gone. The Citizens National Bank closed, and Charles Beckwith was ruined.

## P38 · The envelope opened
`[SHOTS: S10k_opened, S10l_forgeries]` `[PAGE: B05_envelope — the tally stops; a cut line; FORGED]` `[FX: the wax seal cracks]`
At the Wade Park Bank, at last, somebody cut open the brown envelope. Inside were the Carnegie notes. They were forgeries, and not even good ones.

## P39 · The Breslin
`[SHOTS: S10m_breslin, S10n_arrest, S10o_defiant_face]` `[DRAW: X08.2 the money belt]` `[PAGE: B07_newspaper — her 1904 photo prints in; the nicknames type: Queen of Swindlers · Duchess of Diamonds · High Priestess of Fraudulent Finance]` `[PHOTO: chadwick_1904.jpg]` `[FX: snow, flashes]`
She had already fled to New York. On the seventh of December, detectives found her at the Hotel Breslin, in bed, with a money belt strapped around her holding about a hundred thousand dollars. Her husband, the kind doctor, sailed for Europe, and divorced her.

## P40 · The trial
`[SHOTS: S11a_courthouse, S11b_courtroom_lines → S11b_courtroom (draws itself), S11c_defence, S11d_prosecutor]` `[LABEL: "Cleveland · March 1905"]` `[FX: rain and umbrellas, the crowd's murmur]`
Her trial, in Cleveland in March nineteen-oh-five, drew crowds and reporters from across the country. Her lawyer called her a helpless woman. The prosecutor held up the notes.

## P41 · In the gallery
`[SHOTS: S11e_carnegie, S11f_note_pov]` `[DRAW: X05.0 Carnegie (Inset beside the gallery)]`
And somewhere in the gallery, reportedly, sat a small man with a white beard, who had come to see the woman who had made his name worth two million dollars without him ever signing a thing.

## P42 · Guilty
`[SHOTS: S11g_verdict, S11h_verdict_face]` `[DRAW: X09.0 the gavel]` `[FX: stamp 14 YEARS, the gavel]`
The jury found her guilty on seven counts of conspiracy. Fourteen years in prison, and a fine of seventy thousand dollars.

## P43 · The bankers
`[SHOTS: S11i_beckwith_home, S11j_spear]`
The bankers paid too. Charles Beckwith died before he could be tried. His cashier, Spear, was sent to prison for seven years.

## P44 · Her trunks
`[SHOTS: S12a_trunks, S12b_cell]` `[DRAW: X09.1 the trunk]` `[LABEL: "Ohio Penitentiary · January 1906"]` `[FX: breath, snow]`
In January nineteen-oh-six, she arrived at the Ohio Penitentiary for the second time in her life. This time she brought her trunks, her rugs and her furniture, and the warden let her keep them.

## P45 · The end
`[SHOTS: S12c_hospital, S12d_rings]` `[DRAW: X09.3 Cassie in her chair]` `[NOTE 5: her fiftieth birthday]`
It didn't last. In the autumn of nineteen-oh-seven she went blind, and on the tenth of October, she died in the prison hospital. It was her fiftieth birthday.

## P46 · Woodstock
`[SHOTS: S12e_woodstock]` `[DRAW: X09.2 the gravestone]` `[PAGE: B03_map — the whole route in one line, back to Woodstock]` `[FX: leaves falling]`
She was buried in Woodstock, Ontario, the town where, at twenty-one, she had first called herself an heiress.

## P47 · After
`[SHOTS: S12f_tourists, S12g_oberlin_build, S12h_carnegie_reads]` `[NOTE 6: the Oberlin library]`
For years, sightseers came to stare at her house on Euclid Avenue. In Oberlin, a new library went up at the college, paid for by Andrew Carnegie. And Carnegie lived on, one of the richest men in the world, whose name, for two years, had been worth more in Ohio than his money.

## P48 · The paper
`[SHOTS: S12j_mansion_today, S12i_pavement]` `[FX: snow, a gas lamp; the last shot is the pavement and a folded paper]`
She never stole a dollar with a gun, or a lock pick. She used paper, and a name, and a story so big that nobody dared to check it. All it took was one man, picking up one note, on Fifth Avenue.
